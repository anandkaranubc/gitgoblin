# Results, 2026-10-23

Measured with `@gitgoblin/eval` on the dates given; every table can be
regenerated from the files under `data/` with the commands in the README.
Read `design.md` for what each experiment does and `research-2026-10-23.md`
for why.

## E1. Discovery's ranking against newcomer outcomes

**Question.** If discovery had run on 2026-06-20 for the contribute goal, would
its ranking have pointed newcomers at projects where newcomers then got pull
requests merged, better than the conventional rankings?

**Data.** 400 repositories, 100 per language (TypeScript, Python, Rust, Go),
sampled with a fixed seed from five star bands (10 to 49 up to 5,000+),
created before 2026-03-22 and pushed after 2025-12-22. Each was rebuilt as of
2026-06-20 from its GitHub history and scored with discovery's own
`scoreRepo`. Outcomes are newcomer pull requests in the 120 days after: a
newcomer is an outside author whose first pull request in the repository falls
in the window. Grade 0 means none merged; 1, at least one; 2, at least two
with half or more merged; 3, grade 2 with a median first response within a
week. 398 were fetched; 56 whose pull request history could not be fetched
back to the as-of date (busy projects, median 63,000 stars) are left out of
the primary analysis and put back in a sensitivity check; 46 more were
excluded by discovery's own rules at T (no push in six months, issues
disabled), leaving 296.

**Graded outcome** (nDCG@10, macro over languages, paired 95% bootstrap):

| ranking | nDCG@10 | AUC (grade 1+) | difference from gitgoblin |
|---|---|---|---|
| gitgoblin contribute score | 0.571 | 0.83 | |
| most stars | 0.351 | 0.74 | -0.22 [-0.35, -0.05] |
| most open starter issues | 0.303 | 0.56 | -0.27 [-0.41, -0.09] |
| most recent commit | 0.373 | 0.78 | -0.20 [-0.36, -0.01] |
| random | 0.284 | | -0.29 [-0.49, -0.11] |
| newcomer merge rate, prior year | 0.639 | 0.79 | +0.07 [-0.07, +0.28] |

Discovery beats stars, starter-issue counts, recency and chance on this
outcome. Starter-issue counts carry almost no information (AUC 0.56). With
the 56 busy projects put back, which favours stars, the lead over stars
shrinks to 0.18 and its interval touches zero (-0.35 to +0.02); the lead over
starter issues and chance holds.

**Where the successes were.** Success (grade 2 or 3) was rarest under 100
stars (7%) and most common between 1,000 and 9,999 stars (39%); 53 of the 76
successes had no open starter-labelled issue at T.

**A newcomer's odds.** The graded outcome partly rewards busy projects. A
newcomer cares about something else: the chance that their pull request is
merged. Among the 109 repositories with at least three decided newcomer pull
requests in the window, Spearman correlation between each ranking and the
share of those merged:

| ranking | Spearman | AUC (half or more merged) |
|---|---|---|
| gitgoblin contribute score | 0.04 [-0.16, 0.24] | 0.53 |
| most stars | -0.47 [-0.60, -0.31] | 0.22 |
| most open starter issues | -0.08 [-0.26, 0.11] | 0.46 |
| share of all pull requests merged, prior year | 0.50 [0.35, 0.64] | 0.82 |
| newcomer merge rate, prior year | 0.54 [0.38, 0.67] | |

Discovery's score said nothing about a newcomer's odds; stars said the
opposite of the truth. The best predictor was a number discovery already
fetches from ecosyste.ms but folded into "responsiveness" at 40%: the share of
pull requests merged in the past year. The responsiveness signal itself had a
per-language AUC near 0.5 on the graded outcome, though it carried the
highest weight in the profile. (This share counts every author over a year;
intel's `activity.externalPrMergeRate` counts a recent sample of outside pull
requests only, so `discover` and `analyze` can report different shares for
the same repository.)

**What changed on this evidence.** One candidate profile was chosen on this
date: a new `acceptance` signal (the share of pull requests merged in the
past year) at weight 3, and weights moved toward the signals that carried the
outcome here (activity up, bus factor up, responsiveness, docs and license
down). Here it changes nDCG@10 by +0.02 [-0.06, +0.16] and the newcomer-odds
correlation by +0.18 [+0.09, +0.27]. Because the odds outcome was added after
seeing this data, the change ships only if a test written down in
`design.md` before the second date was read passes on 2026-02-15.

**Held-out date, 2026-02-15.** A second pool of 400 (seed 2), the same design,
120-day window; 394 fetched, 60 dropped for coverage, 266 eligible. What
replicated, and what did not:

| finding | 2026-06-20 | 2026-02-15 |
|---|---|---|
| nDCG@10, gitgoblin minus stars | +0.22 [+0.05, +0.35] | +0.16 [+0.03, +0.28] |
| nDCG@10, gitgoblin minus starter issues | +0.27 [+0.09, +0.41] | +0.08 [-0.02, +0.21] |
| gitgoblin against newcomer odds (Spearman) | 0.04 | 0.03 |
| stars against newcomer odds | -0.47 | -0.53 |
| prior-year newcomer merge rate against newcomer odds | 0.33 | 0.27 |
| successes with no open starter-labelled issue | 53 of 76 | 30 of 54 |
| merged outside pull requests that close an issue | 16% | 14% |

The lead over stars replicated; the lead over starter-issue counts shrank and
its interval now includes zero. Discovery's score again said nothing about a
newcomer's odds, and stars again said the opposite.

**The pre-registered test.** On 2026-02-15 the candidate profile improved the
newcomer-odds correlation by +0.105 [+0.017, +0.194] (criterion met) and
nDCG@10 by +0.048 [-0.069, +0.145]; the rule required the lower bound to be
above -0.05, so it failed. The weights did not ship. The `acceptance` signal
shipped unweighted (#113), so `explain` shows a newcomer the share of pull
requests a project merges while every score stays the same. A second
held-out date, written into `design.md` before its data was fetched, decides
the weights.

**Second held-out date, 2025-10-15.** A third pool of 400 (seed 3); 391
fetched, 62 dropped for coverage. The candidate weights changed newcomer odds
by +0.015 [-0.092, +0.112] and nDCG@10 by -0.012 [-0.084, +0.082]: neither
criterion met, so the weights are retired. Without the pre-registered rule,
the design date's numbers (+0.18 on odds) would have shipped a change that
did nothing on a third of the evidence.

Across the three dates, what holds:

| finding | 2026-06-20 | 2026-02-15 | 2025-10-15 |
|---|---|---|---|
| nDCG@10, gitgoblin minus stars | +0.22 [+0.05, +0.35] | +0.16 [+0.03, +0.28] | +0.23 [+0.06, +0.37] |
| nDCG@10, gitgoblin minus starter issues | +0.27 [+0.09, +0.41] | +0.08 [-0.02, +0.21] | +0.21 [+0.09, +0.38] |
| gitgoblin against newcomer odds | 0.04 [-0.16, 0.24] | 0.03 [-0.20, 0.27] | 0.14 [-0.11, 0.38] |
| stars against newcomer odds | -0.47 [-0.60, -0.31] | -0.53 [-0.67, -0.35] | -0.49 [-0.64, -0.29] |
| share of all pull requests merged (`acceptance`) against odds | 0.50 [0.35, 0.64] | 0.32 [0.09, 0.53] | 0.20 [-0.04, 0.42] |
| share of outsiders' pull requests merged, full prior year | 0.51 [0.34, 0.65] | 0.48 [0.28, 0.66] | 0.28 [0.03, 0.49] |
| the same, from the last 50 decided pull requests only (repositories with cached history: 93, 64, 61) | 0.51 [0.35, 0.66] | 0.30 [0.05, 0.52] | 0.21 [-0.05, 0.47] |

- Discovery finds projects where newcomers land work far better than stars,
  on every date, and better than starter-issue counts on two of three.
- Nothing in discovery's score predicts a newcomer's odds of being merged.
  Stars predict them reliably, in the wrong direction: the more popular the
  project, the smaller the share of newcomers' pull requests it merges.
- The share of pull requests a project merges is informative on two dates of
  three. Outsiders' share over a full year is better on all three, but the
  version a query could afford (the last 50 decided pull requests) is no
  better than the share discovery already shows, so `acceptance` keeps its
  current definition.

## E2. Intel opportunities against later changes

**Question.** When intel finds work outside the issue tracker (CI that will
break, dead links, old TODOs, skipped tests, deprecated calls), is that work
real? Proxy: was the flagged line removed later by a commit aimed at it, more
often than a random line of the same file?

**Method.** Each repository was analyzed at its default-branch commit as of
2026-04-25, with the clock set to that date and link and dependency checks
off, then every file-anchored finding was graded at HEAD on 2026-10-23. A
finding counts as acted upon when its excerpt is gone from the file and from
the rest of the tree, and the first commit after T in which it is absent
touched at most 50 files. The control is a seeded random line of at least 12
characters from the same file at T, graded by the same rule. Issue, feature
request, stale pull request, triage and run-based CI findings were dropped,
because they read today's API rather than the past.

Two sets of repositories: the 17 used to calibrate intel (polished, popular),
and 47 drawn at random from E1's candidate pool (star bands from 10 to
5,000+, four languages), which is what discovery actually hands intel.

| kind | pool: findings | acted upon | control | calibration: findings | acted upon | control |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ci_rot (rule table) | 22 | 18% | 9% | 3 | 33% | 0% |
| broken_link | 9 | 22% | 11% | 1 | 1 of 1 | 0 |
| tech_debt (old TODOs) | 32 | 3% | 3% | 15 | 0% | 7% |
| test_gap (skipped tests) | 9 | 0% | 0% | 7 | 43% | 0% |
| deprecation | 3 | 0% | 0% | 1 | 0% | 0% |
| hygiene | 4 | not checkable | | 0 | | |
| all | 79 | 9% | 5% | 27 | 19% | 4% |

By rank within each report, acted upon against control: pool top five 15%
against 8%, ranks 6 to 15 4% against 5%, below 15 0%; calibration top five
60% against 0%, below 15 6% against 7%.

**A third, independent sample** agrees. The intel session graded 19 other
repositories (sqlalchemy, httpie, aiohttp, pip-tools, pip, mitmproxy,
sphinx, jinja, werkzeug, undici, fastify, koa, got, gorilla/mux, urfave/cli,
BurntSushi/toml, serde, clap, caddy) at their 2025-10-23 commit over a year
with the same targeted rule in `packages/intel/scripts/backtest-code.ts`:
ci_rot 40% against a 5% control, broken links 31%, old TODOs 8%, untested
files 0%, deprecations and docs drift 0%.

**Hand-read check.** Every counted item was read against its commit. Pool: 6
of 7 are real (four CI fixes, each a deliberate human change to exactly the
flagged line, such as `macos-14` to `macos-15`; an outsider's fix for a
header-forwarder memory leak that resolved a TODO; a README link), 1 is a
49-file docs rewrite. Calibration: 4 of 5 are real (vite's skipped test,
Pillow's xfail, ruff's link fixed by an outsider, guzzle's `setup-node`
bump), 1 is a rename that touched a skipped test. Treat "acted upon" as about
85% precise. Early versions of the grader counted removals inside 300-file
migrations and re-wrapped comments; the sweep limit, whole-tree search and
whole-token matching exist because of those.

**Who acts, and when.** Outsiders made 2 of the 12 counted fixes; maintainers
made the rest, a median of 124 days (pool) and 156 days (calibration) after
T. Only one of the four repositories with CI fixes runs Dependabot for
actions.

**Baseline: the tracker's own starter work.** In the same repositories, of
the issues carrying a starter label at T, 10% (13 of 133, pool) and 9% (7 of
76, calibration) were resolved by an outsider's pull request in the window,
and about 80% were still open.

**What this says.**

- CI rot and broken links are real demand: acted upon at two to eight times
  churn in all three samples, nearly always by maintainers, months later. An
  outside pull request on them is unlikely to be wasted or raced.
- Old TODOs are not a signal of demand: at churn in all three samples (3%
  against 3%, 0% against 7%, 8% against 5%). They should rank below CI and
  links, which intel's value constants did not do (TODO 0.35 to 0.45 against
  links 0.4 to 0.5).
- Skipped tests split: acted upon in polished projects, not in the pool.
  Untested files and deprecations were not acted upon in any sample.
- The top of intel's ranking is where the action is in both of my samples;
  below rank 15 almost nothing happened.
- Outsiders take labelled issues about four times as often as file-anchored
  work (about 10% against 2%). The tracker is not the whole of the work, but
  it is where outsiders look; non-tracker findings are opportunities with
  little competition rather than proven paths.

**Changes made in intel on this evidence** (by the intel session, measured
here first): the untested-file value went from 0.45 to 0.35 and plain old
TODOs from 0.35 to 0.25, keeping the higher value for TODOs that cite a closed
issue or a met condition; evidence excerpts are now verbatim (a TODO keeps its
tag as written, undocumented-API findings quote definition lines); generated
sources such as jq's flex-built `src/lexer.c` are no longer analyzed; and
vendored directory names, stubs, deferred and compatibility imports, and
deprecated calls in examples and docs no longer produce deprecation findings
(intel pull request #70 and earlier).

**Issue-backed opportunities.** The two runs accepted 607 issue-backed
opportunities. Their fate can only be judged later; `intel issues` records
it against the analysis time and can be re-run in months. One was already
fixed when it was accepted (psf/requests #4664, closing pull request merged
in 2023, ranked first for that repository) and intel's "already fixed?"
check did not flag it.

## E3. Beyond the tracker

**Question.** How much of the work outsiders get merged starts from an issue?

From the pull request and issue histories of E1's 395 repositories over the
year before 2026-06-20:

| language | repositories | merged outsider pull requests | that close an issue | median per repository | issues closed as completed | closed by any pull request | closed by an outsider's |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Go | 98 | 1,543 | 17% | 15% | 1,652 | 38% | 16% |
| Python | 98 | 1,006 | 11% | 12% | 1,925 | 19% | 7% |
| Rust | 99 | 1,705 | 19% | 14% | 1,914 | 41% | 18% |
| TypeScript | 100 | 2,598 | 14% | 8% | 1,908 | 25% | 17% |
| all | 395 | 6,852 | 16% | 13% | 7,399 | 31% | 14% |

"Close an issue" means GitHub links the pull request as closing one, which
needs a closing keyword ("fixes #12"); pull requests that mention an issue
without the keyword are not counted, so 16% is a floor, not a measurement of
tracker-driven work. Even so, the typical repository's merged outside work
mostly did not close an issue, and outsiders closed about one completed issue
in seven. A tool that only shows issues shows a minority of the work that
actually lands.

**What that work was.** Up to eight merged outside pull requests per
repository (1,258 from the 217 repositories that had any), classified by the
files they touched:

| kind | share of merged outside pull requests | closing no issue |
|---|---|---|
| code (tests may ride along) | 47% | 80% |
| mixed (code with docs, CI or manifests) | 33% | 80% |
| docs | 11% | 90% |
| CI and build | 4% | 96% |
| dependency manifests | 4% | 93% |
| tests only | 1% | 94% |

Most outside work is code, and most of it closes no issue. Docs and CI, the
kinds intel looks for outside the tracker, are about a sixth of what lands and
almost never come with an issue. Python projects take the most docs changes
(18%), Rust and Go the most code-only ones (56% and 51%). The sample takes the
same number from every repository, so busy projects count no more than quiet
ones.

## E5. Which open issues do outsiders resolve?

**Question.** Of the issues open on a given day, which ones does an outsider
go on to fix? Is intel's issue classifier a better guide than the labels and
signals people use today?

**Data.** 3,862 issues open on 2026-06-20 in 100 repositories sampled from
E1's pool, each assessed by intel's classifier as it looked that day, and
followed for 120 days. "Outsider fix" means closed as completed by a merged
pull request from someone who is not an owner, member, collaborator or bot.
In 9 busy repositories the stalest open issues were beyond the 200-issue
read limit.

| issues open at T, selected by | issues | repositories | fixed by an outsider | completed by anyone | closed as not planned |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| nothing (every open issue) | 3,862 | 85 | 2.8% [1.8, 3.9] | 14.6% | 4.4% |
| intel accepts it | 1,249 | 65 | 4.9% [2.9, 7.3] | 15.7% | 1.4% |
| a starter label | 104 | 28 | 5.8% [1.1, 10.9] | 8.7% | 1.0% |
| a maintainer commented before T | 1,120 | 69 | 1.8% [0.9, 2.9] | 11.7% | 1.2% |
| opened in the 90 days before T | 680 | 52 | 6.6% [3.7, 9.7] | 27.1% | 13.1% |
| intel calls it triage | 432 | 52 | 0.9% [0.2, 2.0] | 18.5% | 10.2% |

Intel's verdict is about as precise as a starter label and finds twelve times
as many issues in more than twice as many repositories; issues it accepts are
rarely closed as not planned. A maintainer's comment is no guide at all.
Triage candidates are, as intended, not fix work.

**Freshness matters most.** Splitting what intel accepts by the issue's age
at T. This split was found by looking at this date, so it is a hypothesis: a
confirmatory test on 2026-02-15 is written down in `design.md` and has not
been run yet.

| intel accepts it, and the issue was | issues | fixed by an outsider |
|---|---|---|
| opened in the 90 days before T | 206 | 12.6% [5.1, 20.5] |
| older | 1,043 | 3.4% [2.3, 4.6] |
| older than a year | 727 | 2.8% [1.7, 4.1] |

A fresh issue intel accepts was about four times as likely to be fixed by an
outsider as an old one, and twice as likely as a labelled one. Intel's issue
value today only discounts issues idle for years, and reactions, which grow
with age, raise it.

Two corrections from the intel session's own run of this experiment (27
repositories, 2026-05-27) are not yet applied to these numbers. `issueAsOf`
kept today's comment count, update time and assignees, which leaks into the
verdict at T; intel is fixing it. And GitHub reports private organization
members as contributors, and reporters sometimes fix their own issues; the
report now prints a stricter reading that counts authors with four or more
closing pull requests in a window as core and leaves self-fixes out, which
needs this dataset re-run to fill in (it records the reporter and fixer from
now on). Both runs and the confirmatory test reuse the cached responses.

## A pitfall worth recording

The first pool for E1 used `pushed:2026-03-22..2026-06-20` to pin the
candidates to T. GitHub's `pushed` qualifier filters on a repository's
*last* push, so that pool was exactly the set of repositories that stopped
being pushed after T: 478 repositories, 4 newcomer merges in four months
against 1,272 in the year before. The corrected pool uses star bands,
`created:<T-90d` and the open-ended `pushed:>T-180d`, which discovery's
own 180-day rule already implies for any repository eligible at T.
